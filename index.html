1914 – 1918

His probable journey through the war

Only a few documents about Claude himself survive in the family book. The rest of his story can be pieced together from what his battalions did. Each step is labelled to show how sure we are.

Home

  1. 1891 Documented

    Born in Leeds

    Claude is born in Leeds; his birth is registered in the Bramley district in the first quarter of 1891. He grows up with his sisters Mabel, Ethel and Marjorie.

  2. 1

    1911 Documented

    A plumber in Leeds

    The 1911 census records him working as a plumber. The family home is later given as “Crag View,” Spring Hill, Adel.

Leeds Rifles, 1914–1915

  1. 1

    Autumn 1914 Probable

    Joins the Leeds Rifles

    Claude enlists as Private 2962. When the Army renumbered its Territorial soldiers in 1917 he became 265752, a number from the block given to the 7th Battalion, West Yorkshire Regiment — the Leeds Rifles, based at Carlton Barracks. His original number is a little lower than that of the Leeds Rifles VC George Sanders (No. 3203), who enlisted on 9 November 1914, so Claude probably joined in the autumn of 1914.

  2. 2

    1914 – early 1915 His battalion

    Training in Yorkshire

    The 7th Battalion moves to Selby in August 1914, then to Strensall and York, followed by a spell on the Lincolnshire coast.

  3. 3

    March–April 1915 His battalion

    Gainsborough

    The brigade gathers at Gainsborough to prepare for service overseas. The battalion diaries record men “all very keen and cheery and longing for service overseas.”

  4. 4

    14–15 April 1915 His battalion

    To France

    The 1/7th entrains on 14 April, crosses via Folkestone and lands at Boulogne. Whether Claude sailed with them or followed later with a draft of reinforcements is not yet known.

The Western Front with the 1/7th, 1915–1917

  1. 5

    April–June 1915 His battalion

    First taste of the trenches

    The battalion’s first trench tours are near Laventie and Fauquissart, and in May it takes part in the attack on Aubers Ridge.

  2. 6

    July–December 1915 His battalion

    The Ypres Salient

    The battalion holds the line north of Ypres near the Canal Bank, under constant shelling and gas. On 19 December the Germans launch a heavy phosgene gas attack on the Salient.

  3. 7

    1 July 1916 His battalion

    The Somme: Thiepval

    On the first day of the Somme the 1/7th is sent forward from Thiepval Wood to the Schwaben Redoubt. There, Corporal George Sanders wins the Victoria Cross.

  4. 7

    September 1916 His battalion

    Thiepval again

    The brigade attacks near Thiepval on 3 September and supports the capture of the Schwaben Redoubt later in the month.

  5. 8

    October–December 1916 His battalion

    Fonquevillers–Hannescamps

    The brigade takes over a quieter sector north of the Somme for the winter.

Becoming an officer, 1917

  1. 9

    Early 1917 Probable

    Selected for officer training

    Picked from the ranks, Claude probably returns to Britain for the four-and-a-half-month course at an officer cadet battalion. On 1 March 1917 his number changes to 265752 in the Army-wide Territorial renumbering.

  2. 9

    27 June 1917 Documented

    Commissioned Second Lieutenant

    Claude is made a temporary Second Lieutenant in the West Yorkshire Regiment (London Gazette, 24 July 1917). His name appears in the same list as George Sanders VC, who was commissioned from the Leeds Rifles on the same day.

With the 1/6th in Flanders, 1917–1918

  1. 10

    Late 1917 – early 1918 Probable

    Joins the 1/6th Battalion

    Claude is posted to the 1/6th Battalion, which served alongside the Leeds Rifles in the same brigade. We don’t yet know when he arrived: the battalion fought at Poelcappelle, near Ypres, on 9 October 1917 and spent that winter in the Ypres sector.

  2. 11

    April 1918 His battalion

    The German spring offensive

    The Germans break through on the Lys. The 146th Brigade fights in the Battles of Estaires, Messines, Bailleul and the First Battle of Kemmel Ridge. By 24 April the 1/6th holds the line near the Grand Bois and Zero Wood, just north of Wytschaete.

  3. 11

    25 April 1918 Documented

    The Second Battle of Kemmel Ridge

    At about 2.30 a.m. a tremendous bombardment of gas and high explosive begins. At 4.30 the barrage lifts and German infantry attack through thick fog. The front-line companies of the 1/6th are surrounded and fight on at the Grand Bois until the evening. Lieut.-Colonel Wistance and most of Battalion Headquarters are killed; only two officers and forty-four men reach Ouderdom Camp on the night of the 26th. Claude is killed that day, aged 27.

Remembered

  1. 12

    After the war Documented

    Tyne Cot Memorial

    Claude’s body was never identified. The ground was held by the Germans after the battle, and he is named on the Tyne Cot Memorial to the Missing near Zonnebeke, Panels 42 to 47 and 162, among the soldiers of the Ypres Salient who died after August 1917 and have no known grave.

25 April 1918

The day Claude died

This is what happened to Claude’s battalion, the 1/6th West Yorkshires, during the Second Battle of Kemmel Ridge. It is drawn from the regimental history in the family book, which quotes the battalion war diaries and the commanding officers’ reports, and from the battalion’s own history by Captain E. V. Tempest.

  1. 21 April, evening

    The 1/5th West Yorkshires relieve the South Africans in the front line north of Wytschaete. For three days the enemy’s guns and trench mortars harass them, and casualties are “fairly heavy.”

  2. Night of 24/25 April

    The 1/6th, under Lieut.-Colonel W. A. Wistance, comes up from support at Vierstraat and relieves the 15th Durham Light Infantry on the left of the 1/5th. It holds the south-eastern edge of the Grand Bois, from Onraet Farm to Zero Wood, with three companies in the front line (“D,” “B” and “A,” from right to left) and “C” Company in support. The hill of the Grand Bois is described as “the last natural barrier between the Ypres plain and an enemy advance.” The relief is complete by midnight.

  3. 2.30 a.m.

    A tremendous bombardment opens on the whole British line from Bailleul to Voormezeele. An officer of the 1/6th recalled: “Gas shells rained down in thousands, and in a few minutes a thick mist of gas covered the whole forward area. Telephone communications were broken instantly.”

  4. 4.30 a.m.

    The barrage suddenly lifts and passes over the Grand Bois. A few minutes of “uncanny silence” follow. The men move out among the shell holes and wait. There is thick fog, and they cannot see more than a few yards.

  5. 4.40 – 5.40 a.m.

    High-explosive shells “literally rained on the battalion’s front line and heavy casualties were suffered.” Then the guns lift and the German infantry advance in massed waves on the right.

  6. Early morning

    The front companies hold their ground, but in the mist they cannot see that the line on their right has been broken. The first sign is German troops working round behind their right flank. Attacked from both sides by superior numbers, few escape. Captain Saunders VC of “C” Company is seen rallying his men from the top of a pillbox, firing his revolver until he falls wounded.

  7. 6.40 a.m.

    Two exhausted runners reach Battalion Headquarters with news that “C” Company is fighting a rearguard action at Zero Wood.

  8. About 7 a.m.

    The Germans reach the Northern Brickstacks, cutting off all runners to the front line. Colonel Wistance forms his Headquarters staff (signallers, runners and orderlies) in two ranks behind a pillbox. The Adjutant, Captain Mossop, fires S.O.S. signals.

  9. About 9 a.m.

    The fog clears. Machine-gun fire sweeps the valley between the Grand Bois and Vierstraat. The Medical Officer, Captain H. E. Robinson, whose aid post has been blown in, is shot dead carrying a message. Regimental Sergeant-Major Barker is killed emptying his revolver into the enemy. Captain Mossop is wounded and captured. Colonel Wistance is killed by a machine-gun bullet as he rejoins the remnant of his men on the ridge behind.

  10. All day, until 7.30 p.m.

    The front-line companies “D,” “B” and “A” are fired on by machine guns and rifles, shelled by a field gun at almost point-blank range, and machine-gunned from the air, yet they hold the Grand Bois until 7.30 p.m., when they are finally overwhelmed. The regimental history calls it “a splendid instance of indomitable pluck and endurance.”

  11. Night of 26 April

    The survivors of the 1/6th, Lieutenant Maclusky and Second-Lieutenant Hesketh with forty-four other ranks, report at Ouderdom Camp.

  12. Afterwards

    The battalion history records: “Practically no men came back from the three front Companies of the Battalion. Their fate remained a mystery till the end of the war, when prisoners came home and were able to tell their story.” The 1/6th’s losses in the battle were twenty-two officers and 457 other ranks.

Where was Claude?

No account yet found names him. As a Second Lieutenant he would normally have commanded a platoon in one of the four companies. He has no known grave, and almost no one came back from the three front-line companies, so he was most probably with “D,” “B” or “A” Company in the front line at the Grand Bois. Further research may confirm this.
